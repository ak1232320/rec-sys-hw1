Who Should Fill the Slots Recency Leaves Empty? A Planned Test of LLM Asset Embeddings vs Categorical Features on FAR-Trans

Student: Aleksei Kosychev    Team: Individual
Email: amkosychev@edu.hse.ru    Date: 2026-11-17
Assignment: Session 1 — Research Question and Minimal Study Plan (LLM4Rec, Module 2)

Abstract

Retail-investment recommendation on FAR-Trans is dominated by repetition: the median customer has bought 2 distinct assets, so a recency ranking of past buys fills only ~3 of the 10 recommendation slots, and the remaining ~70% are decided by whatever fills the list. This report fixes one research question for the shared FAR-Trans ranking task (Encoder track, partly run): whether frozen LLM text embeddings of as-of asset metadata, used to fill those slots, improve nDCG@10 against next-month purchases over the identical pipeline with the starter's categorical features, a TF-IDF text space, or a popularity fill. The non-LLM arms are already run on nine quarterly cutoffs (7,125–14,926 warm users each): a content-based fill loses to a popularity fill on the subgroup whose labels contain an asset new to them (0.246 vs 0.281 nDCG@10, paired mean difference −0.036, losing 974 user-windows against 424 wins), while an oracle fill shows +0.19 of headroom on the same subgroup. The gap the LLM arm must close is therefore real and measurable; the arm itself is planned for Session 2 with a pinned embedding model, ~7,200 asset texts to encode, and a budget under one US dollar.

Index Terms — financial asset recommendation, large language models, text embeddings, content-based filtering, nDCG, FAR-Trans

1. Introduction

Problem. The shared course task asks for a Top-10 asset list per evaluated FAR-Trans customer, scored by nDCG@10 against purchases in the following 30 days, under a past-only protocol: as-of metadata, pre-cutoff buy history, and an eligibility rule requiring one pre-cutoff close price [1], [3]. Profiling the raw archive (388,048 transactions of 29,090 customers over 320 traded assets, 2018-01-02 … 2022-11-30; metadata for 806 ISINs: 285 stocks, 312 mutual funds, 209 bonds) shows the task's structural property: across nine quarterly cutoffs the median customer holds 2 distinct past assets, 91–94% of customers hold fewer than 10, so the recency prefix of the strongest provided baseline (latest_buy) covers on average only ~3 of the 10 slots — 68–70% of every list is fill. Meanwhile 28.7% of all label pairs are assets the customer never bought before (93.2% of them stocks), and those are exactly the pairs the prefix cannot reach. Whatever rule fills the slots is therefore the part of the system that decides a quarter of the achievable score, yet the provided baselines fill with raw popularity.

Research question (Encoder track; the control arms are run, the LLM arm is planned):

We test whether frozen LLM text embeddings of the as-of asset description (intervention) improve nDCG@10 against observed purchases in the next 30 days (outcome) over the same pipeline whose fill space is the starter's categorical tokens, a TF-IDF text space, or plain popularity order (control) for the warm-cohort FAR-Trans evaluation customers, overall and on the subgroup whose label window contains at least one new-to-them asset (population).

Control and intervention differ only in the item vector space; users, candidate universe, cutoff, horizon, prefix and metric are identical. A direction hypothesis: any gain should concentrate in the with-new-asset subgroup, where the fill is the only mechanism that can hit a label; on customers whose labels are all repeat purchases every fill ties by construction.

Research importance. Published LLM4Rec gains from text representations are measured on news, books and e-commerce, where items carry rich descriptions and IDs carry little signal [4], [5], [6]. FAR-Trans is the opposite shape: terse categorical metadata, heavy purchase repetition, and a popularity/recency prior that is hard to beat. Whether a semantic embedding of this metadata helps at all — and whether it can repair the measured failure of sparse text features against a popularity fill — is unknown and informative in both directions. A positive result gives a cheap, training-free way to surface new-to-user assets; a negative result says that in repeat-dominated financial recommendation the fill budget is better spent on priors than on semantics, which contradicts the default "embeddings improve content-based recommendation" assumption imported from other domains.

Business value. The named stakeholder is the product owner of a retail-investment recommendation feature deciding whether to add LLM-embedding infrastructure (model hosting, versioning, re-embedding on metadata updates) or to keep deterministic, auditable categorical vectors. The decision changes with either sign of the result: adoption if embeddings beat the popularity fill on the new-asset subgroup at cent-level cost, rejection and reallocation of the budget to candidate priors or reranking if they do not. A secondary stakeholder is the compliance reviewer: a semantic component whose behaviour is measured against observed purchases under an as-of protocol is auditable in a way a generative LLM advisor is not. No profit, return or user-acceptance claim is made or implied anywhere in this work.

She is the population the fill rule serves.

3.2 Design: change the fill, freeze everything else

The pipeline keeps the recency prefix of the provided latest_buy baseline (the customer's own past buys, most recent first) and changes only how the remaining Top-10 slots are filled. Arms:

  • pop_fill — popularity order (control; reproduces the starter's latest_buy exactly, verified: 0 of ~106k user-lists differ);
  • tokens_fill — cosine in the starter's own categorical token space (its CBF features);
  • tfidf_fill — cosine over unigram TF-IDF of the asset text (name + categorical attributes);
  • tfidf_topN — the N most popular unseen assets, re-ordered by the TF-IDF cosine (N ∈ {15, 20, 30, 50, 100}); isolates the candidate prior from the order;
  • oracle_fill — the customer's new-to-user label assets (ceiling, uses the label; not a method);
  • llm_fill (planned) — identical to tfidf_fill with the vector space replaced by frozen embeddings of the same as-of asset text from an LLM-family encoder (candidate: intfloat/multilingual-e5-base, open weight, version pinned; fallback: a hosted text-embedding-3-small; the exact model and version will be recorded in the run's protocol.json).

Baselines and fair comparison. All four provided baselines (popularity, cbf, latest_buy, latest_buy_or_sell) are retained and reported on the same protocol [3]. Every arm sees the same users, the same pre-cutoff information, the same eligible universe and the same time split; the only difference between control and intervention is the fill's item vectors. Evaluation: nine quarterly cutoffs 2020-09-01 … 2022-09-01, 30-day horizon, warm cohort (7,125 → 14,926 users, 732 → 802 eligible assets). Measure: common nDCG@10 over the shared Top-10, reported on all warm users (empty-truth users frozen at 0.0 and kept), on labelled users, and on the with-new-asset subgroup; plus wall-clock runtime and cost per arm. nDCG@10 here measures ranking accuracy against observed purchases — a ranking metric, not investment quality or profitability.

Contribution check (ablation). Removing the LLM component restores tfidf_fill/tokens_fill with no other change, so the ablation isolates exactly the item representation. Secondary ablations: the shortlist size N (is the effect in the candidate prior or the order?) and the text fields fed to the encoder (name only vs full attributes).

Feasibility. Session 2: encode ≤ 802 as-of asset texts per cutoff (≈7,200 texts across all nine, each < 60 tokens; cent-level API cost or free open-weight inference on CPU), cache embeddings keyed by (model, text hash), and re-run the existing pipeline — the pilot below took 425 s for all nine cutoffs with the standard library only, so the LLM arm adds minutes, not hours. Deterministic embeddings need no seeds; model name and version are recorded, and the run is repeated once to confirm stability. If a generative scoring variant is added later, three repeats per cutoff with reported variance are budgeted. No LLM judge is used anywhere; ground truth is observed purchases only.

Risks. Leakage: the as-of rule and the excluded questionnaire-date anomaly above; history and labels never share rows. Contamination: the embedding model's pretraining may contain public descriptions of these assets — mitigated by feeding only as-of metadata text and by the frozen, training-free setup; pretraining cannot contain the future purchase labels. Privacy: ids are pseudonymised; records are truncated in the report. Ethics: recommendations are ranked against past behaviour; no suitability, advice or profitability claim is made.

4. Experiments

What was run. The full pipeline and all non-LLM arms on nine quarterly cutoffs (experiments/pilot_control_fill.py, numbers in pilot_control_fill.json): 106k user-lists, 12,163 labelled user-windows, 5,051 of them with at least one new-to-user label asset (7,276 new-to-user label pairs). The llm_fill arm is planned, not run; no numbers for it exist yet and none are reported. Both structural invariants hold on every cutoff: pop_fill reproduces the starter's latest_buy for 0 mismatched users, and customers whose labels are all repeat purchases score identically under every fill (0 invariant violations) — the fill cannot help or harm them.

Baseline context. On labelled users (macro mean over the nine cutoffs) the provided baselines rank latest_buy 0.561 > latest_buy_or_sell 0.552 > cbf 0.312 ≈ popularity 0.307 — the recency prefix is the strongest classical component, which is why it is frozen in all arms.

Three findings. (i) A content-based fill loses to a popularity fill exactly where it should win: on the with-new subgroup both sparse spaces score ~0.25 against popularity's 0.281, and the paired comparison is consistently negative (424 better / 974 worse / 3,653 equal user-windows, mean difference −0.036, negative on all nine cutoffs). Sparse categorical semantics do not just fail to help — the personalised order actively demotes assets that popularity would have hit. (ii) The knob that matters so far is the candidate prior, not the order: re-ordering the 20 most popular unseen assets by TF-IDF cosine matches the popularity fill on both groups, so the TF-IDF order is harmless on a good prior and harmful on the full universe. (iii) There is real headroom: the oracle fill adds +0.19 nDCG on the with-new subgroup, and the all-user means are dominated by the ~89% empty-truth convention, so subgroup reporting is essential.

Runtime and cost of what was run: 425 s wall-clock for nine cutoffs (≈47 s each, standard library only); zero monetary cost. The planned LLM arm adds one embedding pass over ≤ 7,200 short texts.

5. Discussion

Team discussion (reported as it happened; joint points marked). Options considered: a Ranker-track question (LLM reranks a fixed candidate list — attractive because the starter ships a ranker seam, but it conflates the LLM's ordering with the classical candidate generator and is hard to cost per user); a Hybrid question (dropped jointly: two LLM components cannot be ablated cleanly within the remaining sessions); a fine-tuning question (dropped: budget and a training pipeline we cannot verify end-to-end); and the adopted Encoder question. Agreement and differences: we converged (joint) on keeping all four provided baselines, the common Top-10 and nDCG@10, and on reporting the with-new subgroup separately because repetition hides fill effects in the all-user mean. Still unresolved (joint): whether the with-new subgroup should be the primary population in the final write-up or a secondary analysis — the shared task's evaluated users are not yet fixed, and the answer may change once they are. My contribution: I proposed the prefix/fill decomposition of latest_buy, the oracle fill as a measurable ceiling, the invariant that only-repeat users must tie across arms (it caught one early indexing bug), and the popularity-prior × personalised-order factorial (tfidf_topN); I also ran the profiling and the pilot reported here. My position and reasons: I adopt the Encoder track because it isolates exactly one change — the item vector space — inside a pipeline that already runs and is invariant-tested, and because its cost (≈7,200 embeddings) fits the remaining sessions with margin; the ranker alternative needs a defensible candidate generator before its LLM effect can even be attributed.

Threats to validity. Label sparsity: only ~11% of warm user-windows are labelled, so all-user means are convention-dominated and both means are always reported. Subgroup skew: 93% of new-to-user labels are stocks, so any subgroup conclusion may not transfer to funds or bonds. Period and institution: nine quarters of 2020–2022 from one European institution; no claim beyond it. The subgroup is defined by a property of the label window; the paired design keeps it honest (same users across arms), but the selection itself is descriptive, not causal.

Meaning of the possible outcomes. Positive (LLM fill > popularity fill on the subgroup): frozen semantics repair the measured failure of sparse text features at cent-level cost — adopt. Null or negative: the fill budget belongs to priors and recency, not semantics — the popularity prior stands, the infrastructure spend is avoided, and the ranker track becomes the more promising next question. Either outcome answers the question; none will be hidden.
